The problem, the answer, the proof

This project is real and ongoing. I manage it end to end, from requirements gathering to production rollout and the sales presentation. Anonymised presentation: no real company, supplier, site or amount; the numerical examples come from the demo dataset, which is entirely fictitious. Code not public.

A Procurement department receives dozens of price reviews every year, in every format (Excel, CSV, PDF). Today, the buyer spends 1 to 3 days per review reformatting the file, matching items to the ERP and quantifying the impact — and often negotiates on the increase advertised by the supplier.

The project supports two processes: analysis of supplier price lists (from receipt to negotiation file) and quarterly supplier scorecards (no more re-keying; the buyer keeps only the judgment calls).

For recruiters · business value
  • End-to-end Project Manager on a real project: requirements, specifications, architecture, development, user acceptance testing, deployment, go-to-market.
  • Quantified business case in three scenarios (low / central / high): from 0.5% to 2.3% of tracked spend, payback from the first quarter.
  • Failure conditions spelled out in black and white: unreliable volumes, unmatched master data, unfavorable balance of power, alerts not followed up.
  • Proposed organization: a team of 8, including two business profiles deemed essential to success.
For tech teams · what the code proves
  • Python 3.12 engine driven by YAML configuration: adding a supplier is a matter of configuration, not code.
  • Matching cascade L0 exact → L1 rules → L2 fuzzy (rapidfuzz) → L3 human review, versioned and reversible master data.
  • DuckDB warehouse (≈ 120 tables, 91 interface views under a versioned contract), read-only R/Shiny interface, 8 access profiles enforced server-side.
  • Quality: ≈ 3,850 Python tests (pytest-xdist), ≈ 800 R tests (testthat), GitHub Actions CI Python 3.12 / 3.13 + ruff, access control tested.
  • Guiding principle: what the engine cannot read stays empty, with its reason — never zero, never guessed.

// As-is / to-be process

From the supplier file to the negotiation memo

Today (as-is)
  • Manual reformatting and item matching: 1 to 2 days.
  • Year-over-year comparison and impact quantification: 0.5 to 1 day.
  • Arguments and memo: 0.5 day.
  • Checking that the price is applied in the ERP: rarely done.
  • Negotiation on the average increase announced by the supplier.
Target (to-be)
  • File uploaded as is; template recognized and remembered: 30 min.
  • Automatic matching; the buyer only reviews doubtful cases.
  • Volume-weighted increase, annualized impact, Pareto: automatic.
  • One-page memo and Excel support produced: 15 min.
  • ERP price application gaps detected and quantified every month.

Example from the demo (fictitious data): a price list announced at +4.80% only weighs +2.08% once weighted by volumes and the discount grid — and the impact comes down to a handful of items.

// Business case

Six gain levers, quantified and bounded

#LeverOrder of magnitudeWhat makes it credible
G1Negotiate on the volume-weighted increase, not on the advertised average0.3 to 0.8 pt of increase avoided on the spend under reviewQuantified argument, item by item
G2Pay the agreed price: gap between price paid in the ERP and price due0.10 to 0.50% of spendSeparates a simple delay in applying the price from a truly unexplained gap

Gains expressed as a share of tracked spend. They always separate what is measured (on the demo) from what is estimated (assumptions to be confirmed on real data), and the method for replacing estimates with actual figures from the first campaign onward is documented.

Prerequisite for every other gain: reading prices without error. Replaying a corpus of price lists revealed ten silent reading errors (an ignored “price per 1,000” basis = a price a thousand times too low; a US-format date = a three-month gap). Each one now has its own regression test.

// Risks & success conditions

What would make these figures wrong

Identified failure conditions
  • Unreliable ERP volumes: the weighting collapses (the tool then refuses to quantify).
  • Unmatched master data: no comparison possible — the first workstream, in buyer time.
  • Unfavorable balance of power: the tool provides the argument, not the leverage.
  • Alerts not acted upon: a gap that is detected but never corrected yields nothing.
Deployment plan (6 months, 8 work packages)
  • L0 scoping and access → L1 ERP connection → L2 master data migration → L3 user acceptance testing on real data.
  • L4 interface and access profiles → L5 savings and negotiation → L6 industrialization → L7 rollout and training.
  • Go / no-go milestones: one entity connected (M2), one supplier validated end to end by the buyer (M3).
  • Functional scope frozen, documentation delivered with each work package.

Step by step

  1. Upload — the file is received as is, fingerprinted and archived; a duplicate is rejected.
  2. Understand — template recognized, unique key detected, reading report presented before any calculation.
  3. Match — each supplier item is linked to the ERP item through the cascade; the buyer only reviews doubtful cases, once.
  4. Analyze — weighted increase, annualized impact, Pareto, discounts, special price caps, ERP application gaps; outliers go to quarantine.
  5. Negotiate — one-page memo, Excel support and correction request; every figure points back to its source.

// Critical review

Known limitations and next steps

Documenting limitations honestly is part of the job: it lets a team make informed decisions.

  • Containerized multi-user deployment (B-92) and corporate directory integration still in the backlog.
  • Known and tracked defects: B-123 (links wrongly suspended), B-127 (savings and quarantine), B-119 (interface).
  • Only one rich industry profile (industrial) besides the generic profile; interface in French.
  • Gains remain estimates until the first campaign on real data has taken place — the measurement method is ready.
  • Critical review of business rules completed: critical defects identified (price tiers, discounts, encodings) and fixed one by one, each with a dedicated test.
