Power BI: is this supplier price increase justified?

End-to-end Power BI project for industrial buyers: it checks each price list against actual raw material prices (INSEE indices), quantifies what can be renegotiated, alerts before the supplier does, checks that every site of the group pays the best price and measures what negotiation actually brought in. Model generated by script as PBIP / TMDL, 103 DAX measures, row-level security, machine learning tested against a naive baseline.

// In 30 seconds

The problem, the answer, the proof

Every year, suppliers announce an increase “because of raw materials”. Buyers often negotiate blind: no quantified link between the requested increase and the actual change in the materials the product is made of, decreases rarely passed on (ratchet effect), a supplier who knows the prices first, and, in a group that has acquired sites, the same item paid more at one site than another.

Buyvera BI follows the questions a buyer asks before a supplier meeting, in order: detect, quantify, target, understand, negotiate, then align prices across the group and prove the savings. Demo: real INSEE raw material indices; prices, volumes, sites and suppliers are fictitious, generated with a fixed seed.

For recruiters · business value
  • A business question before the data: the logic comes from 20 years of B2B industrial field experience.
  • A journey, not a wall of numbers: 5 steps that follow how a supplier meeting is prepared, down to the counter-offer.
  • Complete demo: from the supplier's increase letter to the counter-offer, then the savings, on a fictitious dataset.
  • Honest about ML: the project shows where machine learning adds nothing, and proves it with a backtest.
For tech teams · what the code proves
  • PBIP / TMDL star schema fully generated by script and versioned in Git: 26 tables, 26 one-to-many single-direction relationships, 13 pages.
  • Item × material bridge table without bidirectional relationships: justified increase computed item by item, then volume-weighted.
  • Python precomputation of increases by item × campaign × window × lag: measures answer in one second, to the cent.
  • Row-level security (Buyer and Management roles, USERPRINCIPALNAME()), tested; every table, column and measure documented.
  • Blocking checks, DAX tests on Power BI Desktop's local engine, Power BI vs Python cross-check with zero gap on 5 key figures.

// Machine learning

What history can really predict

Use caseEvaluation methodBuyer use
Raw material indices at 1-6 months (INSEE, real)LightGBM and smoothing compared with the naive forecast (“next month looks like last month”): the naive one wins and is kept, with a range drawn from past errorsJustifiable ceiling for the next campaign, with its range
Volumes by product familyHolt-Winters compared with the seasonal naive, kept only if it does betterQuantities to buy ahead of the increase
Supplier segmentationKMeans, checked against the behaviors injected into the demo dataTarget opportunists and ratchet effects
Abnormal price linesIsolation Forest, alerts checked against supplier profilesLines to check first

Every model goes through a chronological backtest (retrained at each date on past data only) against a naive baseline, and is kept only if it beats it. On metal prices, no model does better than the naive one: the tool says so, and gives a range rather than false precision.

Step by step

  1. Real indices — 7 monthly INSEE series (copper, aluminum, nickel, zinc, ferrous metals, naphtha) fetched through the SDMX API.
  2. Demo data — 3,000 items, 10,142 price lines, 62,095 purchase lines; requested increase = justified increase + supplier profile bias + noise.
  3. Precomputation — increases by item, group view over 3 sites, negotiation results; then machine learning (forecasts, segments, anomalies).
  4. Checks — injected behaviors automatically recovered, DAX tests, Python recompute of the key figures.
  5. Power BI — model and report generated by script as PBIP, opened in Power BI Desktop without a Pro license.

  2. 02

// Critical review

Known limitations and next steps

Documenting limitations honestly is part of the job: it lets a team make informed decisions.

  • Prices, volumes, sites and suppliers are fictitious: the amounts illustrate how it works, not a client result.
  • No orchestrator or continuous integration: the pipeline is a sequence of scripts run in order.
  • Runs in Power BI Desktop; publishing to the Power BI service (scheduled refresh) remains to be done.
  • Stated assumption for the non-material share (labor, energy): +2% per year.
